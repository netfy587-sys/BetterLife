A guide to reading the night sky

Everything up there is visible before you ever buy a telescope

A page on naked-eye astronomy — how to find your way around the sky, which constellations to start with, and what actually needs equipment versus what doesn't.

Where to begin

You don't need a telescope to start. On a clear night away from city lights, the unaided eye can pick out roughly two and a half thousand stars, five planets, and the pale band of our own galaxy overhead.

The only real requirement is patience — about twenty minutes of darkness for your eyes to fully adjust, and a red flashlight if you need to check a map without ruining that adjustment.

What to look for tonight

MOON

Phase

The phase changes visibility for fainter objects — a full moon washes out the sky, while a new moon is best for deep viewing.

PLANETS

Wandering stars

Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn are all bright enough to see without help — the giveaway is that they don't twinkle like stars do.

ISS

The space station

Visible as a steady, fast-moving point of light for a few minutes after sunset or before sunrise, on the right pass.

Four to start with

Orion

The hunter — look for the three-star belt, visible worldwide in winter evenings.

Ursa Major

The Big Dipper — a reliable starting point for finding Polaris, the North Star.

Cassiopeia

A clear W shape — easy to spot and directly opposite the Big Dipper across Polaris.

Cygnus

The Northern Cross — sits along the Milky Way's band, best seen in summer skies.

Sharing the dark

Dark-sky etiquette

  • Use red light only

    White light ruins night vision for everyone nearby — a dim red flashlight preserves it.

  • Give eyes and telescopes room

    Stray light and vibration both interfere with someone else's view — keep distance and a low profile.

  • Ask before using a scope

    It's common courtesy at public viewing spots to ask before looking through someone else's equipment.
